In the heart of the Southwest, where red rocks and towering saguaros dominate the skyline, water is a precious and transformative force. While Arizona is famous for its arid landscapes, those who know where to look will find hidden grottos, turquoise falls, and perennial creeks that defy the desert heat.

If you’re looking to trade the dusty trails for a day by the water, here are the must-visit water hikes in Arizona- and the essential permits you’ll need to access them.

1. Seven Sacred Pools (Sedona)

Located along the Soldier Pass Trail, these tiered pools carved into the red sandstone are one of Sedona’s most iconic sights. While they are technically “ephemeral” (filling mostly after rain), they create a stunning visual contrast against the vermilion cliffs.

  • The Hike: A moderate 1.1-mile trek from the trailhead.
  • Permits: No hiking permit is required. However, the Soldier Pass Trailhead parking is extremely limited and often closed. You must use the free Sedona Shuttle (Thursdays–Sundays) or obtain a motorized permit via Recreation.gov if you plan to drive a 4×4 to the pools.

2. West Fork of Oak Creek (Oak Creek Canyon)

Often cited as one of the best hikes in the United States, West Fork winds through a narrow “sub-canyon” with towering walls and a gentle, crystal-clear stream.

  • The Hike: 6.6 miles round-trip, featuring 13 easy stream crossings.
  • Permits: No permit is required, but there is a daily parking fee at the Call of the Canyon Day Use Area. This lot fills up by 8:00 AM during peak season, so arrive early!

3. Fossil Creek (Camp Verde
Strawberry) – Pictured at The Top of This Post

Fossil Creek is a lush riparian paradise where calcium-rich water creates stunning travertine dams and deep turquoise swimming holes.

  • The Hike: Most visitors take the 2-mile (round-trip) Waterfall Trail to the main falls.
  • Permits:Strictly Required. From April 1 to October 1, you must reserve a seasonal parking permit in advance via Recreation.gov. These are released on the first of each month and often sell out within minutes.

4. Havasupai Falls (Grand Canyon)

The “Holy Grail” of water hikes. Located on the Havasupai Indian Reservation, these world-famous blue-green waterfalls (Havasu, Mooney, and Beaver Falls) are a bucket-list destination for hikers globally.

  • The Hike: A strenuous 20-mile round-trip backpacking trek. Day hiking is strictly prohibited.
  • Permits:Mandatory and Competitive. You must secure a 3-night/4-day permit through the official Havasupai Reservations website. Permits for the 2026 season typically go on sale in early February and cost approximately $455 per person.

5. Parsons Trail in Sycamore Canyon (Clarkdale)

Sycamore Canyon is the “quieter cousin” to the Grand Canyon. The Parsons Trail takes you deep into a wilderness area where Parsons Spring provides a perennial flow of water even in the driest months.

  • The Hike: 8 miles round-trip through a lush, shady canyon floor.
  • Permits: No permit is required for hiking, but because it is a designated Wilderness Area, group sizes are limited and mechanized transport (including bicycles) is prohibited.

6. Oak Creek Powerline Trail (Sedona)

For those looking to escape the Slide Rock crowds, the Powerline Trail offers a more rugged way to access the deeper pockets of Oak Creek. It’s a great spot for birdwatching and finding a quiet “beach” to yourself.

  • The Hike: Approximately 3–5 miles depending on your turnaround point.
  • Permits: A Red Rock Pass (available at kiosks or online) is required for parking at most trailheads in the Sedona/Oak Creek area.

7. The Cresent Moon Ranch (Sedona)

Nestled along the banks of Oak Creek in the heart of Sedona, the area around The Moon Ranch offers a rare, tranquil riparian experience. Unlike the high-traffic swimming holes, this stretch of the creek feels like a private sanctuary where the lush canopy of sycamores and cottonwoods provides deep shade even in the height of summer.

  • The Hike: A gentle, scenic walk along the creek bed with multiple opportunities for “creek hopping.”
  • Permits: This area is unique because parts of it border private land. While the creek itself is public, you must be careful to stay within the forest service boundaries. A Red Rock Pass is required for parking at nearby trailheads like Chavez Crossing.

8. Cibecue Falls (White Mountains
Salt River Canyon)

If you’re looking for a “mini-Havasupai” experience without the 20-mile hike, Cibecue Falls is a showstopper. The trail requires several knee-deep crossings through a dramatic, narrow canyon before ending at a powerful 30-foot waterfall.

  • The Hike: 4 miles round-trip. You will be in the water for much of the hike, so sturdy water shoes are a must.
  • Permits:Strictly Required. This hike is on White Mountain Apache Tribal Land. You must purchase a Special Use Permit ($45) online in advance through the WMAT Game & Fish website. Note: No swimming is allowed at the falls out of respect for the land.

9. Wet Beaver Creek – “The Crack” (Rimrock)

“The Crack” at Wet Beaver Creek is the ultimate desert swimming hole. The creek has carved a deep, narrow slot into the red rock, creating a natural diving platform and a long, deep pool of clear water.

  • The Hike: A flat but exposed 7-mile round-trip trek (Bell Trail).
  • Permits: No permit is required, but the parking lot at the Bruce Brockett trailhead fills up very quickly on weekends.

Important Safety: The Flash Flood Warning

Arizona water hikes come with a unique risk: Flash Floods. Rain falling miles away can send a wall of water through these narrow canyons with almost no warning. Always check the weather forecast for the entire drainage area, not just the trailhead, and never enter a canyon if rain is predicted.

🎒 The Water Hike “Essentials” List

Before you head out, your pack for a water hike should look a bit different than a standard Grand Canyon trek:

  • Footwear: Choose shoes with “sticky” rubber soles (like Five Ten or specialized water hikers). Standard flip-flops will slip on mossy rocks.
  • Dry Bags: Protect your keys, phone, and camera. Even a “shallow” crossing can result in a slip.
  • Quick-Dry Fabrics: Avoid denim or heavy cotton. Stick to synthetics that won’t chafe when wet.
